Point 10 – Town Hall (Palazzo Frizzoni)

Historical Itineraries 2.0

Historical context

Reaching Piazza Giacomo Matteotti, we come across the town hall building known as Palazzo Frizzoni.

This building, a symbol of municipal power, became the official seat of the city administration on October 28, 1928 (the anniversary of the March on Rome ), replacing the former town hall located at Via T. Tasso, 4. The choice of this date was not accidental: October 28, 1928 marked the celebration of Mussolini’s rise to power and of the fascist regime, and represented a moment of consolidation of central government control over local administrations. Palazzo Frizzoni thus became the symbolic place of fascist power in the city. At the head of the municipal administration was the podestà, a figure who replaced the mayor, and one of the most well-known podestàs of that period was Antonio Locatelli, who held the office during those years.

The March on Rome marked the beginning of the fascist regime and became an opportunity to legitimize central government control over local administrations.

Why it is a place of memory

From 4 February 1926, all functions of mayor, city council, and municipal board were centralized and assigned to the podestà, a figure that echoed medieval models of centralized government, admired by Mussolini, who reinterpreted them as instruments of autocracy and control.

The podestà remained in office for a period of five years, but a subsequent fascist law established that only those who were already married could hold this office, which excluded Antonio Locatelli from continuing his mandate. This episode represents an emblematic example of the punitive and rigid regulations imposed by fascism, which established rules not only political but also social and personal, in a perspective of total control over private lives. Palazzo Frizzoni is therefore a witness of an era in which local autonomy was replaced by the fascist dictatorship.

The municipality was no longer an expression of the citizens, but an instrument of the fascist State.

In-depth – Antonio Locatelli

Antonio Locatelli was born in 1895 and took part in the First World War as an aviator, distinguishing himself for his courage and flying skills. The war experience deeply marked his life and helped strengthen his bond with nationalist ideology, which in the following years led him to enthusiastically embrace fascism.

During the rise of the fascist regime, Locatelli began a political career: between 1924 and 1928 he was elected as a deputy to the Parliament of the Kingdom of Italy. In 1933 he began his activity in Bergamo by taking on the role of podestà. However, his mandate lasted only one year: in 1934, following a law wanted by Mussolini that prevented unmarried men from holding public office, Locatelli was forced to leave the position.

On January 7, 1936, he volunteered for the war in Ethiopia, taking part in military operations as an aircraft pilot. During the conflict, despite the technological and military superiority of the Italian army, Ethiopian forces managed to organize effective resistance actions. On a night in June 1936, the Ethiopians attacked an Italian airfield, setting the aircraft on fire: all the soldiers present were killed in the attack, including Antonio Locatelli.

His death was immediately celebrated by the regime as a heroic sacrifice. In Bergamo, a true cult of the hero developed, fueled by fascist propaganda, which presented Locatelli as a symbol of courage, dedication to the homeland, and loyalty to fascism. Even today, in the tower located in Piazza Vittorio Veneto, Antonio Locatelli is remembered through a sculpted portrait, a testimony to historical memory and to the significance his figure had for the city.

“La rivista di Bergamo,” a newspaper used for Bergamasco propaganda during the dictatorship, dedicated the entire July–August 1936 issue to the memory of Antonio Locatelli. pp. 367–413
