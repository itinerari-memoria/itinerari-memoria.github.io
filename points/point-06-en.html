06 - Donadoni Middle School – Women in arms

Historical Itineraries 2.0

Historical context

We are in Via Torquato Tasso 14, where today the “Eugenio Donadoni” Middle School stands. During the Italian Social Republic (1943–1945), this building was not a school: it housed a unit of the GIL (Gioventù Italiana del Littorio – Italian Youth of the Lictor) and a detachment of the Female Auxiliary Service (SAF), the voluntary female corps of the RSI. The SAF was officially established by Decree no. 447 of 18 April 1944, in the period following the Armistice of 8 September 1943, when Italy was split in two: on one side the South, allied with the Anglo-Americans and initially led by the Badoglio government; on the other, the Italian Social Republic, supported by Nazi Germany and established in the northern part of the country. In this local context, the SAF counted around 130 volunteers, young Italian women between the ages of 18 and 40. The command was based in the Casa del Fascio, the political and military coordination center of Bergamo, where tasks were assigned and training courses organized. During these courses, the young volunteers were trained with discipline and competence to carry out assistance, logistical, and frontline support duties. The women of the Female Auxiliary Service were not intended for direct combat: their role was to support the military structures of the Italian Social Republic. They carried out assistance services in hospitals, office and clerical work at headquarters, propaganda activities, and logistical tasks such as providing refreshments for troops. Through these roles, the regime sought to sustain the war effort and free men to be deployed at the front.

Why it is a place of memory

SAF volunteers were motivated by loyalty to the regime, love of the homeland, and a spirit of adventure, but also by the desire to break away from traditional female roles. They attended national and provincial training courses characterized by strict discipline and military-style preparation, and carried out support duties such as cleaning, cooking, nursing, typing, and switchboard operations. At the end of the courses, they took an oath according to the formulas of the RSI armed forces and, although they did not fight with weapons, they were considered militarized personnel with the status of war volunteers. Participation was significant: between May 1944 and April 1945, 5,771 applications were recorded, with 1,016 auxiliaries active by July 1944 and a total of 4,412 volunteers before the corps was dissolved. The uniform consisted of a long skirt, military jacket, and a beret bearing a symbol composed of the fasces, the central emblem of the Fascist regime borrowed from ancient Rome, where lictors carried it before magistrates as a symbol of authority and power. During Fascism, the fasces appeared everywhere: on flags, public buildings, uniforms, and official documents, evoking discipline, order, and loyalty to the State. Combined with military elements on the auxiliaries’ beret, it symbolized the authority of the regime and the bond between citizen and State. A coat and long stockings completed the uniform; discipline demanded rigor, hierarchical respect, and comradeship. The SAF represented a model of the Fascist “militant woman citizen”: active in service to the Fatherland and loyal to the regime, yet confined to auxiliary roles and subordinate to male military authority.

According to historian Isabella Manchia, an expert in the study of the role of women in the Italian Social Republic, “The image produced by that myth was that of the auxiliary: a young woman, driven by ardent patriotic faith, unarmed and non-violent, who sacrifices herself for the homeland. However, this stereotype served to channel all female activity in the Italian Social Republic into subordinate roles, erasing any form of autonomy or real decision-making power.”

In depth: Lydia Among the Nations

Lydia Gelmi Cattaneo was born in Presezzo in 1902 and lived at Valverde Castle. The daughter of a medical officer, passionate about culture, a miniaturist and among the first women to obtain a driving license in Bergamo, she stood out for her courage and generosity. During the Second World War, between 1943 and 1945, she saved numerous Jews, including Irene Weiss, by hiding and protecting them from Nazi-Fascists. She also helped partisans and escaped prisoners, risking her own life to defend those who were persecuted. For her commitment, she became the first woman from Bergamo to be awarded the title of “Righteous Among the Nations”, a recognition granted to those who save human lives at the risk of their own. Her life remains an example of courage, humanity, and civic responsibility for all humanity.
