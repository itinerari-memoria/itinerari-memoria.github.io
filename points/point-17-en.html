17 - Via Pignolo 13

The Turani group

Historical context

We are in Via Pignolo 13, which was not just a simple address, but one of the operational centers of the Resistance in Bergamo. In these buildings, among hidden courtyards and basements, the lives of those who had chosen to rebel against the regime intersected. After 8 September 1943, the city became a labyrinth of spies and informers: a single whispered word to the wrong person was enough to end up in the hands of the Fascist Federal Police, whose offices were located just a few steps from here.

Why it is a place of memory

The Turani Group takes its name from Arturo Turani, a central figure of the first organized Resistance formation in the city, initially known as the Matteotti Brigade. Composed mainly of young people, it operated in the heart of Via Pignolo and played a fundamental role in clandestine struggle: the recovery of weapons and ammunition — also crucial for other partisan formations — the distribution of anti-Fascist press, the connection with groups active in the mountains and on the plains, as well as support for escaped prisoners and persecuted individuals through forged documents and escape networks toward Switzerland. Its activity was intense but short-lived. Between late 1943 and early 1944, the group was severely struck due to infiltration and betrayal. Arrests led to the dismantling of the group and the execution by firing squad of Arturo Turani, Giuseppe Sporchia, and Cesare Consonni, while other members were imprisoned or managed to escape. Despite repression, the sacrifice of its members made it possible to safeguard other cells of the urban Resistance, which survived and continued the struggle until Liberation.

The words of Arturo Turani offer a direct and deeply conscious testimony of the meaning of the partisan choice. They clearly reveal the renunciation of private life and of the dearest personal bonds, accepted in the name of a higher moral and civic duty. Turani wrote: “I do not spend words to explain how I ended up in this inglorious end, but you know the facts that pushed me to act, and with a clear conscience I can still say today that it was the duty of charity toward one’s neighbor and then the duty of being Italian to act so that the name may not be just a word but a faith of patriotic feeling. Remember me with the same affection with which I loved you, and with the hope given to us by faith I leave you in the name of Christ. Arturo.” In this statement, devoid of any rhetoric, the deepest meaning of sacrifice emerges: separation from loved ones, awareness of death, and the conviction of having acted out of responsibility toward others and toward the country. It is the expression of a Resistance lived as an ethical choice even before a political one. Alongside these words stands the memory of those who survived the repression of the Turani Group: “Betrayal shattered our ranks, but it did not extinguish the idea.” An idea that continued to live on beyond defeat and violence. Even today, passing in front of number 13 on Via Pignolo, the warning left by those who lived through those years still resonates: “Freedom is like air: one realizes how valuable it is only when it begins to be lacking.” A message that remains relevant, inviting us to recognize in the freedom we enjoy the result of painful choices, personal sacrifices, and a profound sense of collective responsibility.

In depth: Arturo Turani

Although not directly part of the Turani Group, Arturo Turani played a fundamental role in the Resistance in Bergamo. Born in Bergamo on 29 September 1888, an architect by profession, after 8 September 1943 he actively joined the War of Liberation, committing himself to organizing an initial partisan nucleus of the “Matteotti” Brigade, a formation that, after his death, would be renamed the “Arturo Turani” Brigade in his honor. On 15 November 1943 he was arrested in his home in Via Pignolo during a clandestine meeting. Handed over to the German authorities, he was tried by a German military court on charges of partisan activity and concealment of weapons, and sentenced to death. The execution by firing squad took place on 23 March 1944 at the “Seriate” barracks in Bergamo. His body was subsequently buried hastily in a field in Grumello del Piano; in August of the same year, his remains were accidentally discovered next to those of his comrade Giuseppe Sporchia and provisionally reburied in the cemetery of Grumello. The sacrifice of Arturo Turani is ideally linked to the Turani Group and to other partisan groups active in the city: both contributed, each in their own way, to organizing the Resistance and protecting partisan cells until Liberation. On the sixtieth anniversary of Liberation, the city of Bergamo chose to honor his memory with a plaque at the site of his arrest, as a testament to his commitment to freedom.
