09 – Piazza Vittorio Veneto

Employment Offices

Historical context

Continuing along Via T. Tasso, we arrive at Piazza Vittorio Veneto, where the ninth of the 17 points of our journey is located: the Employment Offices.

Public institutions responsible for managing the hiring of workers. During the Fascist period they became tools of political control, through which the regime decided who was allowed to work.

During the Italian Social Republic ( RSI ), these offices took on a central role in strengthening Fascist control over the country’s economic and social life. Several offices were established here to manage and allocate labor, with the aim of ensuring that hiring followed Fascist criteria and that workers were aligned with the regime’s ideals. Many workers were sent to Germany as laborers, officially on a voluntary basis, but often under pressure or coercion from the regime. They were mainly employed in work camps and factories, rather than concentration camps, although conditions were still harsh.

In addition to the employment offices, the General Labour Inspectorate also operated in this area, an institution established and managed by the Italian Social Republic.

Fascist state established in Northern Italy between 1943 and 1945 after the Armistice of 8 September. It was led by Benito Mussolini and militarily supported by Nazi Germany.

Why this is a place of memory

Fascist control over workplaces was not limited to bureaucratic organization, but also resulted in discrimination against those who did not support the regime. Starting on 29 March 1928, Fascism directly intervened in the workplace through a Royal Decree, which established that hiring had to prioritize those holding the membership card of the National Fascist Party (PNF). This law not only imposed ideological selection but also excluded non-loyal individuals from employment opportunities.

A decree issued by the King on the proposal of the Fascist government, which introduced direct State intervention in labor relations, favoring workers enrolled in the party.

Document certifying membership in the Fascist Party, often required to obtain or keep a job and access public positions.

“Employers are forbidden from hiring unemployed workers not registered with employment offices” - Art. 11 Decree

Deep dive - The General Labour Inspectorate

The General Labour Inspectorate had two main tasks:

The first was to replace conscripted workers with voluntary labourers. The Inspectorate sought to limit direct coercion by providing “voluntary” manpower in place of requisitioned workers. However, this voluntariness was often only apparent, as many accepted due to necessity, fear of retaliation, or to avoid deportation.

German authorities imposed compulsory labour, forcing individuals to report for forced work with no possibility of refusal.

Constraint or pressure that forces someone to act against their will.

The second objective was direct collaboration with German authorities for infrastructure and strategic works, such as the construction and repair of roads, bridges, railways, and other civil and military works essential for the war effort and territorial control.

In this context, the Organisation Todt also operated, a German organization responsible for major military and infrastructure works in occupied territories during the war. In the province of Bergamo, Todt conscripted 6,848 workers, but only 146 actually reported; of these, 13 were sent to work in Germany.

The harshness of repression is still remembered today by a plaque located in Via Porta Dipinta in Bergamo, commemorating five citizens from Bergamo executed in Pesaro, victims of violence and reprisals linked to the occupation and collaboration system.
