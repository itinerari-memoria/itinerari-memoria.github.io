09 – Piazza Vittorio Veneto

Employment Offices

Historical Context

Proceeding along Via T. Tasso, we reach Piazza Vittorio Veneto, where we find the ninth of the 17 points of our journey: the Employment Offices.

Public agencies responsible for managing the employment of workers. During Fascism, they became tools of political control, through which the regime decided who could work.

During the Italian Social Republic ( RSI ), these offices took on a central role in strengthening Fascist control over the country’s economic and social life. Several offices were established here to manage and employ the workforce, with the goal of ensuring that hiring occurred according to Fascist criteria and that workers were aligned with the regime's ideals. Many workers were sent to Germany as laborers, officially on a voluntary basis, but often under pressure or coercion imposed by the regime. They were primarily employed in labor camps and industries, not concentration camps, though the conditions were still harsh.

In addition to the employment offices, the General Inspectorate of Labor also operated in this area, an agency established and managed by the Italian Social Republic.

A fascist state established in Northern Italy between 1943 and 1945 after the armistice of September 8. It was led by Benito Mussolini and supported militarily by Nazi Germany.

Why is it a place of memory?

Fascist control over workplaces was not limited to bureaucratic organization; it also resulted in discrimination against those who did not follow the regime. Starting on March 29, 1928, Fascism began to intervene directly in workplaces with a Royal Decree, which stipulated that hiring should prioritize those who held the Party membership card of the National Fascist Party (PNF). The law not only established an ideological selection but also helped exclude from employment those who were not loyal to the regime.

A measure issued by the king at the suggestion of the Fascist government that introduced direct state intervention in labor relations, favoring workers who were members of the party.

A document that certified membership in the Fascist Party, often essential for obtaining or maintaining a job and for gaining access to public positions.

In-depth - The General Inspectorate of Labor

The General Inspectorate of Labor had two main tasks:

The first task was to replace drafted workers with volunteer workers. The Inspectorate sought to limit direct recourse to coercion by offering “voluntary” labor in place of requisitioned labor. However, this voluntariness was often only apparent, as many accepted out of necessity, fear of retaliation, or to avoid deportation.

The German authorities imposed “precettazione,” which meant the obligation to report for forced labor, with no possibility of refusal.

Coercion or oppression that forces someone to act against their will.

The second goal was direct collaboration with the German authorities for the execution of infrastructure and strategic works, such as the construction and repair of roads, bridges, and railways, along with other civil and military works critical to the war effort and territorial control.

In this context, the Todt Organization also operated, a German organization tasked with carrying out major military and infrastructure projects, active in occupied territories during the war. In the province of Bergamo, Todt requisitioned 6,848 workers, but only 146 actually showed up; of these, 13 were sent to work in Germany.

The harshness of the repression is still remembered today by a plaque located in Via Porta Dipinta, Bergamo, commemorating five Bergamaschi citizens shot in Pesaro, victims of the violence and reprisals linked to the occupation and collaboration system.
