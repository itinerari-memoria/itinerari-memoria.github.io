12 - Palace of Freedom

Home, school, temple

Historical context

We are standing in front of the Palace of Freedom, a building characterized by an architectural structure organized according to principles of rationality and symmetry, typical of the Fascist period. Built between 1937 and 1940 based on a design by Alziro Bergonzo, it was the Casa del Fascio, the headquarters of the National Fascist Party. This building did not only serve an administrative function, but also represented the center of local political power and was a tool of regime propaganda, designed to make the authority of the State visible in the daily lives of citizens. Inside were the offices of the Federale, the political secretariat, and the assembly hall (now an auditorium).

During the final phase of the Second World War and the Italian Social Republic, the basement of the building was also used as a place of detention. After 1945 it was renamed Palace of Freedom, marking the transition from dictatorship to democracy and temporarily hosting the local CLN.

Offices of the Federale:
The Federale’s offices were those of the provincial leader of the National Fascist Party. The Federale was the most important party figure at the provincial level: he represented Fascist power in the territory and was responsible for controlling local politics, organizing propaganda, and coordinating all party activities.

Why it is a place of memory

The Palace of Freedom is a place of layered memory, as it preserves both material and symbolic traces of different phases of twentieth-century Italian history. During the Fascist period it represented the “House” of the regime and therefore a space of repression, also emphasized by celebratory inscriptions and decorative elements that contributed to building consensus and shaping collective memory. The dedication to Antonio Locatelli, described as “three-time gold medalist,” and the presence of ritual spaces such as the shrine and the assembly hall strengthened the symbolic and almost sacred dimension of political power.

The change of name and function after the war did not erase the material traces of the past, but reversed their meaning: from a symbol of oppression to a sign of the democratic reclaiming of spaces of power.

Today the building invites critical reflection on the relationship between architecture, power, and memory, showing how places are never neutral, but carry political and civic values that can be reinterpreted over time.

Further insight: architectural notes

The Palace of Freedom, now protected as a site of historical and artistic interest, features a rationalist and monumental architectural style, with simple yet imposing forms designed to convey order, strength, and authority. Architecture becomes a tool of propaganda, capable of transmitting the regime’s values even without words. The façade is characterized by a large portico with pillars and a balcony used for public speeches, an element that allowed direct contact between power and the population and had strong symbolic value.

The square in front is also an integral part of the design: it is not just an urban space, but a place intended for gatherings and public events, where the direct relationship between power and the masses is realized. This reflects a typical conception of the period, in which the city is transformed into a scenographic space for political representation.

Decorations, both internal and external, such as frescoes, sculptures, and bas-reliefs, have an ideological function, as they spread values such as courage, sacrifice, and national belonging. In this way, architecture, art, and urban space work together to create a coherent system of communication of power.
