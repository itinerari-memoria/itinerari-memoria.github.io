Point 08 – Ciro Caversazzi Library

Itineraries of Memory 2.0

Historical context

During the twenty years of Fascist rule, public communication was progressively reduced to a single point of view: the press lost its autonomy and became a central instrument for building consensus. Control of public opinion included both censorship (what could not circulate) and propaganda (what was meant to “fill” the public space).

After 8 September 1943, with the German occupation and the birth of the Italian Social Republic, the stakes of information became even clearer: the Resistance understood that without a network of news and clandestine newspapers it would be difficult to organize the struggle, involve the population, and counter the official version of events.

Illegal press also circulated in Bergamo: according to reconstructions of communication during the Liberation struggle, at least two clandestine sheets spread in the city, “Bergamo proletaria” and “Italiani che si liberano”, produced and distributed in extremely risky conditions, among improvised print shops, couriers, night postings, and networks of solidarity.

They were recounted after Liberation. Explore the front page of the newspaper of 27 April 1947 that reconstructs their story.

Why it is a place of memory

The Ciro Caversazzi Library (Via Torquato Tasso 4) is a place of memory because it recalls, in a concrete way, the link between freedom, circulation of ideas, and democracy. If during the war and occupation information could become a terrain of repression and control, today a public library represents the opposite: open access to sources, the possibility of debate, and education in critical thinking.

This meaning is not abstract: the history of the Bergamo Resistance is woven through people and groups who were committed precisely to the spread of clandestine press. Salvo Parigi, for example, worked in the clandestine network of Giustizia e Libertà and initially focused on the dissemination of “Italia Libera”, the newspaper of the Action Party; he was arrested by the SS a few days before the uprising of 25 April 1945.

The SS (Schutzstaffel, “Protection Squads”) were the main paramilitary, political, and police organization of the Nazi Party, founded in 1925 and placed under the command of Heinrich Himmler. From Hitler’s personal bodyguard, they developed into a “state within a state,” becoming the central executive arm of racial ideology and Nazi terror. The SS ran concentration and extermination camps, carried out secret police and repression operations, and were responsible for countless crimes against humanity during the Second World War.

Local sources also recall episodes of repression linked to the circulation of clandestine material: Evaristo Locatelli, belonging to the “Banda Turani,” collaborated in the distribution of clandestine press, was arrested in the raid of 19 November 1943, and suffered torture and abuse in places of detention and interrogation. In parallel, Memoria Urbana reconstructs how the Turani band combined demonstrative actions with propaganda and the production of informational material, later hit hard by Nazi-Fascist repression.

Today this place continues to “work” on memory through public cultural practices: meetings, readings, and initiatives in collaboration with organizations such as ANPI and ISREC (for example, reading groups dedicated to the Resistance hosted in the library courtyard). Memory here is not only remembrance: it is a civic exercise.

In depth

Communication and Resistance: why “without newspapers” it was not enough to fight

In the Liberation struggle, communication had strategic value: informing meant breaking isolation, making occupation and violence visible, connecting different groups, and building trust. The clandestine press, though produced in very difficult conditions, was an essential tool to tell reality “from below” and counter the version imposed by power.

In the case of Bergamo, some reconstructions recall the circulation of illegal sheets such as “Bergamo proletaria” and “Italiani che si liberano”, testifying to a concrete need: creating counter-information in the heart of a controlled and militarized city.

Local episodes: clandestine press, networks, and repression (1943–1944)

Bergamo sources show how the clandestine press was tied to networks of people, couriers, contacts, and everyday “micro-actions”:

  • Salvo Parigi worked on the dissemination of “Italia Libera” (Action Party) and on liaison and information services within the Giustizia e Libertà formations.
  • In the Banda Turani, alongside demonstrative actions, there was propaganda work and the production of informational material; Nazi-Fascist repression hit the group hard between November 1943 and March 1944.
  • The story of Evaristo Locatelli shows in a stark way the risk tied even to the “simple” distribution of clandestine press: arrest, torture, trial, and deportation (with penalties and even death sentences for other members of the band).

Connecting these episodes to a library today helps clarify one point: freedom of the press is not “natural” nor guaranteed once and for all. Public cultural places become spaces where we can remember, understand, and recognize the signs of new forms of control or manipulation.

Why a library also speaks of freedom of the press

A public library is a democratic institution because it allows access to diverse sources, verification, comparison of interpretations, and the building of informed opinions. In a historical context where the press could be controlled and counter-information persecuted, the library represents the civic response: pluralism, shared knowledge, and critical education.

Who was Ciro Caversazzi

Ciro Caversazzi (1842–1906) was a significant figure in Bergamo’s cultural life between the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Recalling his name means recalling a precise idea: culture as a public good and as a lever for emancipation, not as a privilege.
