08 – Ciro Caversazzi Library

The press

Historical context

We are in Via Torquato Tasso 4, in Bergamo, in a building that today houses the "Ciro Caversazzi" library and other municipal offices. It is now a public space for study, reading, and meeting, but during the crucial years of war and occupation this site became home to newspapers reflecting the political changes of the time.

The building, constructed between 1855 and 1858 in neoclassical style to a design by architect Francesco Lodi, originally housed administrative and judicial functions; from 1873 it became the seat of the "Old Town Hall".

Old Town Hall:
After its construction in the nineteenth century, the building was adapted for municipal functions and became the seat of Bergamo's city administration.

Institutional role:
The city council met here from 1873 to 1933.

Historical significance:
This use shows the building's central role in the political and civic life of the city between the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, before its transformation into a public place of culture.

After 25 July 1943, the building hosted "La Voce di Bergamo"; after 8 September 1943, with the German occupation and the Italian Social Republic, the palace became the seat of "Bergamo Repubblicana"; after Liberation the paper took the name "Il giornale del popolo" and became the organ of the provincial CLN.

"La Voce di Bergamo":
During the Fascist period it was one of the most widely circulated newspapers in Bergamo and the main local Fascist daily.

After 25 July 1943:
While keeping the same title, it became the unofficial organ of the inter-party committee and was edited by Alfonso Vajana.

After 8 September:
Publication under that management ended on 10 September; collaborators who failed to escape were subjected to arrests, sentences, and persecution.

Italian Social Republic (RSI):
This was the Fascist regime established in Northern Italy after 8 September 1943, under German control.

Context:
In this phase Northern Italy experienced German military occupation, repression, civil war, and Resistance.

Relation to the press:
Information was placed under strict control and used as a tool of propaganda, political legitimation, and consensus-building.

CLN - National Liberation Committee:
This was the political body coordinating anti-Fascist forces during the Resistance.

After Liberation:
"Il giornale del popolo", directed by Alfonso Vajana, became the organ of the provincial CLN until 31 July 1946.

Significance:
The shift from a Fascist newspaper to a CLN newspaper shows concretely how changes in political power also change the public voice claiming to describe reality.

This chapter belongs to the broader context of Fascist control of information: the monopoly of the press and the importance assigned to propaganda were typical features of both Fascist and Nazi dictatorships. The regime progressively built a system that subordinated newspapers to political power through laws, prior controls, and daily directives.

This system also relied on the Press Law of 1926, the role of the Ministry of Popular Culture, and the system of "veline".

Press Law of 1 January 1926:
It established that no newspaper could be printed without a person in charge recognized by the state authority, locally the Prefect.

Responsibility:
The editor was criminally responsible for everything printed in the newspaper.

Prior control:
A copy of everything printed in typographies (pamphlets, posters, books, newspapers, magazines, and so on) had to be deposited in court before distribution, in order to obtain authorization for printing and circulation.

Ministry of Popular Culture:
This was the main instrument of the regime's new policy of control over the press and propaganda.

Origin:
It emerged from the transformation of the Head of Government's Press Office into the Undersecretariat for Press and Propaganda in September 1933, then became a ministry under its new name on 25 July 1935.

Function:
This ministry oversaw propaganda activities in every sector, as well as the control of the daily press and the press more generally.

Veline:
With the establishment of the ministry, a system of daily instructions sent to editors through confidential circular letters was introduced.

Origin of the name:
The directives were written on thin translucent sheets without letterhead or signature; hence the term "velina".

Function:
They indicated what to publish, what to suppress, and how to frame events, turning the press into an instrument controlled by the regime.

Why it is a place of memory

This place is important because it shows concretely how political power can seize information. In the same building, within just a few years, different newspapers followed one another, each corresponding to a different political moment: from the Fascist newspaper, to the organ of the inter-party committee, to the daily of the Italian Social Republic, and finally to the newspaper of the CLN.

The Ciro Caversazzi Library is a place of memory because it makes visible the relationship between the press, propaganda, and power. Here we understand that information is not neutral: it can be a space for comparison, but it can also become an instrument of control, censorship, and guidance of public opinion.

Remembering this building also means understanding how Fascism built its domination not only through repression of opponents, but also through the disciplining of public communication, the monopoly of news, and the systematic use of propaganda.

The significance of the site becomes even stronger if we think of its present use: a public space devoted to reading and knowledge, that is, the opposite of a press subordinated to power. The library therefore represents a symbolic transformation: from a place of information control to a place of free access to knowledge.

In this building, the history of the press shows how, when power changes, the public voice claiming to describe reality changes as well.

In-depth

The press in Bergamo during the Fascist period

In Bergamo the most widely circulated newspapers during Fascism were "La Voce di Bergamo", the local Fascist daily, and "L'Eco di Bergamo", the Catholic daily expression of the Bergamo Curia. The latter gradually abandoned its support for the Popular Party and also its initial condemnation of Fascism, progressively adopting an attitude of submission to the appeals of ecclesiastical authority and the pressure of the local Fascist organization.

The monopoly of information and the importance attributed to propaganda techniques were typical features of both Fascist and Nazi dictatorships. Propaganda, conducted with new methods suited to the characteristics of modern society, became one of the decisive tools of these dictatorships.

Mussolini, control of newspapers, and the "veline"

Mussolini understood very well the political value of the press, also because of his own experience as editor of "Avanti!" and "Il Popolo d'Italia", and he had in mind an organic plan for subordinating newspapers to Fascism.

Once the regime had consolidated, the repression of opponents was accompanied by the daily adaptation of the press to directives issued by government bodies on every matter considered to be of public interest. In this way the daily press was progressively bent to the needs of power.

From 25 July 1943 to Liberation

The collapse of Fascism on 25 July 1943 caused a series of changes in editorial leadership and newsrooms across the daily press. Many papers kept their old titles, also because the Badoglio government, in addition to introducing prior censorship, banned the publication of new newspapers.

In Bergamo, after 25 July, "La Voce di Bergamo", while keeping its title, became the unofficial organ of the inter-party committee under the direction of Alfonso Vajana. After 8 September the situation changed again: during the RSI the newspaper became "Bergamo Repubblicana", the daily of the Republican Fascist Federation, directed by Arturo Abati.

After Liberation it took the new name "Il giornale del popolo" and, under Vajana's direction, served as the organ of the provincial CLN until 31 July 1946.

Communication and Resistance: why "fighting without newspapers" was not enough

In the struggle for Liberation, communication had strategic value: informing meant breaking isolation, making occupation and violence visible, linking different groups, and building trust. The clandestine press, though produced in extremely difficult conditions, was an essential tool for telling reality "from below" and countering the version imposed by power.

In Bergamo, some reconstructions recall the circulation of illegal sheets such as "Bergamo proletaria" and "Italiani che si liberano", testifying to a concrete need: to create counter-information in the heart of a controlled and militarized city.

Local episodes: networks and repression (1943-1944)

Bergamo sources show how closely the clandestine press was tied to networks of people, couriers, and everyday micro-actions:

  • Salvo Parigi worked within the clandestine network of Giustizia e Liberta and was involved in distributing "Italia Libera", the newspaper of the Action Party; he was arrested by the SS a few days before the uprising of 25 April 1945.
  • Within the Banda Turani, alongside demonstrative actions, there was also work of propaganda and the production of informational material; Nazi-Fascist repression hit the group hard between November 1943 and March 1944.
  • The story of Evaristo Locatelli shows the risk connected even to distributing clandestine press: arrest, torture, trial, and deportation.

SS:
Short for Schutzstaffel, the paramilitary and police organization of the Nazi regime.

Role:
During the war it was involved in political repression, the persecution of opponents, and crimes against humanity.

Historical context:
In occupied territories and in the Italian Social Republic, the SS collaborated with Nazi-Fascist authorities in the fight against the Resistance, in arrests, torture, and deportations.

Historical significance:
Remembering the role of the SS helps us understand the violence of the Nazi system of repression and the price paid by those who opposed occupation and dictatorship.

Linking these episodes to a present-day library clarifies one fundamental point: freedom of the press is neither natural nor guaranteed forever. Public cultural places therefore become spaces where people can remember, understand, and train critical thinking.

Who was Ciro Caversazzi

Ciro Caversazzi (1865-1947) was an important figure in Bergamo's cultural life between the nineteenth and twentieth centuries: a man of letters, scholar, and promoter of civic and cultural initiatives.

According to local archival summaries and catalogues, from 16 May 1945 he became extraordinary commissioner of the Civic Library; he died in Bergamo on 1 May 1947. Naming the library opened in 1948 after him meant linking this place to a local cultural memory close in time to the events of the war and the post-war period.
