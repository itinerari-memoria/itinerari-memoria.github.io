05 – “Giacomo and Pio Manzù” Art High School

The special tribunal

Historical context

We are at 18 Torquato Tasso Street, where the “Giacomo e Pio Manzù” State Artistic High School is currently located, there used to be a building that housed the offices of the Special Tribunal for the Defense of the State.

Institution: Created in 1926 by the fascist regime after the so-called “fascist laws,” it became operational in 1927 to target political opponents and suppress democratic freedoms. It was abolished in 1943 and later re-established by the Italian Social Republic until the Liberation.
Function: It judged so-called “political crimes,” including anti-fascist activities, the dissemination of underground press, membership in banned parties, and offenses against the regime. The defense of the State was synonymous with the defense of fascism.
Composition and trials: It was largely composed of military personnel and regime members. Trials were swift, with few guarantees for defendants and severe limitations on defense and appeal. Verdicts were often predetermined.
Punishments: It imposed prison sentences, internal exile, forced labor, and in some cases the death penalty. The consequences often extended to the social and professional lives of the accused.
Historical significance: The Special Tribunal turned justice into a tool of political repression. Today it is remembered as a symbol of the denial of the rule of law and a warning about the dangers of dictatorships.

The Special Tribunal was established by the fascist regime on November 25, 1926, and began operating in 1927. After an initial suppression in July 1943, it was re-established by the Italian Social Republic and remained operational until the Liberation, with a branch also in Bergamo.

During the 1930s the Special Tribunal assumed an increasingly central role in the repressive system of the fascist regime. With the 1931 extension its powers were expanded: it was no longer limited to repressing anti-fascism, but intervened more broadly in the defense of the “personality of the State” and the security of the regime.

The extension broadened the tribunal’s powers: it was no longer limited to the repression of anti-fascism, but also operated more generally in the defense of the State and the security of the regime.

Although it represented the judicial peak of political repression, the tribunal did not operate alone but within a system that included the Ministry of the Interior, the police, the carabinieri, and the Fascist Party, often with overlapping responsibilities. Within this system, the Special Tribunal mainly judged the most serious and symbolically significant political crimes, while everyday surveillance and preventive control remained in the hands of the other institutions.

Why it is a place of memory

The Special Tribunal represents one of the most repressive tools of the fascist dictatorship. Created after the introduction of the “fascist laws,” it was used to target any form of political opposition, erasing fundamental freedoms such as freedom of the press, association, and the right to strike.

The trials were swift and lacked guarantees: the defendant often didn’t know the charges, couldn’t appeal, and could even be sentenced to death. The verdicts were often pre-determined, making the tribunal a tool of fear and control.

The Special Tribunal shows the true face of the dictatorship: a state that used the law not to guarantee justice, but to suppress dissent.

Further insight: after the Liberation

After the fall of fascism and the end of World War II, places like this lost their repressive function. In the climate of democratic reconstruction of the Italian Republic, the building was repurposed for educational use, becoming the headquarters of the Artistic High School, founded in the early 1960s. The decision to transform a place linked to control and repression into a school reflected the desire to return these spaces to the community and to affirm new values based on education, culture, and freedom.

The subsequent naming of the school after Giacomo and Pio Manzù reinforces the symbolic meaning of this transformation: from the headquarters of a tribunal that limited thought to a place dedicated to creativity and artistic expression. Today, studying in that building means inhabiting a space that tells the story of Italy’s transition from dictatorship to democracy, turning a place of repression into a place of formation and freedom.
