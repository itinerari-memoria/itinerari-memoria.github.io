18 - via Pignolo 10

Ferruccio Dell'Orto

Historical context

We are at Via Pignolo 10. During the Nazi-Fascist occupation, this corner of Bergamo marked an invisible boundary between everyday life and terror. Just a few steps away was the headquarters of the GNR (National Republican Guard), the police of the Fascist regime, composed of Italians who chose to collaborate with the German occupiers. Their units were infamous for the brutality with which they hunted partisans and for the torture inflicted in the basements of the nearby barracks in Via Francesco Nullo.

The GNR (National Republican Guard), established in 1943 by the Republic of Salò, was the armed force composed of Italians who remained loyal to Fascism and the Germans. In Bergamo, the GNR acted not only as a military force but as a political police. Its tasks included population surveillance, hunting draft dodgers, and above all dismantling urban partisan cells. Their main base was the barracks in Via Nullo, which became notorious as a place of detention and torture. GNR militiamen knew the territory as well as the partisans, making the conflict fratricidal and ruthless: they used informers and spies to identify GAP members, applying brutal interrogation methods to extract names and addresses of clandestine bases. The GNR represented the last stronghold of a regime that, feeling besieged, responded with widespread violence to suppress any desire for freedom before it could turn into open insurrection.

Why it is a place of memory

Right here, on February 8, 1945, the story of a 17-year-old boy ended. Ferruccio Dell'Orto, student at the Vittorio Emanuele Institute (code name "Marco"), was exactly where you are now performing a highly risky action: disarming a Fascist officer to seize his weapons. A shootout ensued: Ferruccio was hit in the leg and fell to the ground. Instead of receiving medical care, he was dragged by GNR militiamen to the nearby barracks. In critical condition, he was subjected to inhuman torture, yet Ferruccio chose complete silence. He died that very evening within the walls of the Nullo barracks, less than a hundred meters from where you stand, without betraying his comrades with a single word.

The quote from Piero Calamandrei — “If you want to go on pilgrimage to the place where our Constitution was born, go to the mountains where partisans fell, to the prisons where they were imprisoned, to the fields where they were hanged” — helps to grasp the profound significance of places like this, which are points where freedom was paid for with suffering and life.

In depth: The GAP in Bergamo

The GAP (Patriotic Action Groups) represented the boldest and riskiest component of the Resistance. Unlike the Garibaldi Brigades or the "Justice and Freedom" formations operating in the Bergamo valleys, the GAP operated in the heart of the city, between Città Alta and historic neighborhoods like Via Pignolo. They were organized in small autonomous cells of 3 or 4 people to minimize the risk of infiltration. Their mission was the "wearing down" of the enemy: sabotage of telephone and railway lines, disarming officers to recover weapons, and targeted attacks on command centers. Their strength lay in invisibility: GAP members were often workers or students, like Ferruccio Dell'Orto, who lived seemingly normal lives while striking in the shadows. This choice entailed extreme psychological isolation, as, unlike partisans in the mountains, they had no safe territory or uniforms: in case of capture, they were treated as snipers, not prisoners of war.
