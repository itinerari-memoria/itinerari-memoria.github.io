10 – Palazzo Frizzoni

City Hall Palace

Historical context

Reaching Piazza Giacomo Matteotti, we find the town hall building known as Palazzo Frizzoni.

This building, symbol of municipal power, became the official seat of the city administration on 28 October 1928 (anniversary of the March on Rome), replacing the former headquarters located at Via T. Tasso 4. The choice of this date was not random: 28 October 1928 marked the celebration of Mussolini’s rise to power and the Fascist regime, and represented a moment of consolidation of central government control over local administrations. Thus, Palazzo Frizzoni became the symbolic seat of Fascist power in the city. At the head of the municipal administration stood the podestà, who replaced the mayor, and one of the most notable podestàs of that period was Antonio Locatelli, who held the office during those years.

The March on Rome marked the beginning of the Fascist regime and became an opportunity to legitimize central government control over local administrations.

Why this is a place of memory

Starting from 4 February 1926, all functions of mayor, municipal council, and city board were centralized and assigned to the podestà, a figure inspired by medieval models of centralized government, admired by Mussolini and reinterpreted as tools of autocracy and control. This reform gave the podestà exclusive power to appoint and manage municipal offices, eliminating any form of popular participation.

The podestà remained in office for five years, but a later Fascist law established that only married men could hold this position, which excluded Antonio Locatelli from continuing his mandate. This episode is a clear example of the rigid and punitive regulations imposed by Fascism, which governed not only political but also social and personal life, aiming at total control of individuals’ existence. Palazzo Frizzoni thus bears witness to an era in which local autonomy was replaced by Fascist dictatorship.

The municipality was no longer an expression of citizens, but an instrument of the Fascist State.

Deep dive - Antonio Locatelli

Antonio Locatelli was born in 1895 and participated in the First World War as an aviator, distinguishing himself for his courage and piloting skills. His wartime experience deeply shaped his life and strengthened his connection to nationalist ideology, which later led him to enthusiastically join Fascism.

During the rise of the Fascist regime, Locatelli pursued a political career: between 1924 and 1928 he was elected deputy to the Parliament of the Kingdom of Italy. In 1933 he began his activity in Bergamo as podestà. However, his term lasted only one year: in 1934, following a law promoted by Mussolini that banned unmarried men from holding public office, Locatelli was forced to resign.

On 7 January 1936 he volunteered for the Ethiopian War, taking part in military operations as an air pilot. During the conflict, despite the technological and military superiority of the Italian army, Ethiopian forces organized effective resistance actions. One night in June 1936, Ethiopian fighters attacked an Italian airfield, setting aircraft on fire: all soldiers present died in the attack, including Antonio Locatelli.

His death was immediately celebrated by the regime as a heroic sacrifice. In Bergamo, a true cult of the hero developed, fueled by Fascist propaganda, which presented Locatelli as a symbol of courage, dedication to the homeland, and loyalty to Fascism. Even today, in the tower located in Piazza Vittorio Veneto, Antonio Locatelli is remembered through a sculpted portrait, a testimony to historical memory and to the significance his figure had for the city.

"La Rivista di Bergamo", a newspaper used for propaganda in Bergamo during the dictatorship, dedicated the entire July–August 1936 issue to the memory of Antonio Locatelli pp. 367–413
